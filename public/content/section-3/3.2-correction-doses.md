A **correction dose** is an extra dose of rapid-acting (bolus) insulin taken to bring high blood sugar levels back into a safe target range between meals or overnight. 

Even with precise carbohydrate counting, factors like stress, illness, hormone shifts, or miscalculated portion sizes can cause blood sugar to drift above target. A correction dose acts as a resetting mechanism to safely bring glucose levels down.

---

### What is an Insulin Sensitivity Factor (ISF)?

To calculate a correction dose correctly, you must know your **Insulin Sensitivity Factor (ISF)**, also referred to as a **Correction Factor**.

Your ISF tells you **how many mmol/L (or mg/dL) one unit of rapid-acting insulin will lower your blood sugar**.

* **Determining Your ISF:** Your ISF is calculated with your diabetes care team based on your total daily insulin requirements.
* **Example ISF:** An ISF of **2.0 mmol/L** means 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin will reduce your blood sugar reading by 2.0 mmol/L.

---

### Critical Safety Rule: Avoid "Insulin Stacking"

The most common mistake when taking correction doses is taking them too frequently, leading to a severe low blood sugar event (hypoglycaemia). This is called **insulin stacking**.

* **Rapid-acting insulin takes time:** Fast-acting insulin takes **3 to 5 hours** to clear your system completely. It reaches peak effectiveness around 1 to 2 hours after injection.
* **Wait between doses:** Unless advised otherwise by your care team, avoid taking another correction dose within **3 to 4 hours** of a previous rapid-acting dose. 
* **Insulin on Board (IOB):** If you use a Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) app, smart insulin pen, or insulin pump, pay close attention to "Insulin on Board" (or active insulin), which automatically tracks how much unused insulin remains in your body.

---

### When to Take a Correction Dose

* **With Meals:** Add the correction dose to your mealtime carbohydrate insulin dose if your blood sugar is high before eating.
* **Between Meals:** Administer a standalone correction dose if your blood sugar remains unexpectedly high hours after eating, provided no active insulin remains from your last dose.
* **Check Ketones First:** If your blood sugar is **above 13.0–15.0 mmol/L**, check for ketones before taking a correction dose. High ketones may require a larger correction dose or additional medical guidance.
